## A. The shot (most weight)

### A1. Shot size - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_(filmmaking)

- Shot size is how much of the subject the frame holds: from an extreme wide that shows a whole setting down to an extreme close-up that crops to a detail.
- The field of view comes from lens angle plus distance; in a 2D motion stage the equivalent is the scale of the world inside the frame.
- Each size has a job: the wide places things, the medium relates a subject to what is near it, the close-up isolates one thing.

### A2. Shot types and when to use them - https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/ultimate-guide-to-camera-shots/

- Meaning comes from combining sizes, angles and moves; a film that never varies size has no way to signal what matters.
- An extreme long shot makes a subject feel small, distant or isolated; use it for loneliness or for scale.
- A wide shot keeps the subject readable inside its surroundings; the establishing shot exists to set geography before detail.
- Size contrast between consecutive beats is itself a signal: the cut from wide to close says "this one thing now".

### A3. Camera angle - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_angle

- A high angle shrinks a subject (weak, small, vulnerable); a low angle makes it big (strong, threatening).
- Very wide views are usually taken from above so the viewer reads the layout.
- Most shots sit at eye level; an angle change should mean something, not decorate.

### A4. Composition - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composition_(visual_arts)

- Lines, including the edges between colours and a row of repeated elements, steer the eye through the image.
- Value contrast is what makes form read; the strongest contrast in the frame is where the eye lands first.
- Positive and negative space are both designed; empty space around a subject gives it weight.

### A5. Rule of thirds - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_thirds

- Place key elements on the third lines or their crossings rather than dead centre.
- Off-centre placement adds tension and energy; centring reads as calm, stable, final (useful for a logo end card).

### A6. Mise-en-scene - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mise-en-sc%C3%A8ne

- Everything placed in the frame (set, objects, light, colour, positions and movement) is the director's statement.
- Colour can carry state of mind: bright and warm for one mood, dark and cool for another.
- Composition includes how objects are positioned and how they move within the shot.

### A7. Low-key lighting - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-key_lighting

- A high lighting ratio (one key light, deep shadow) shapes contours and builds mood.
- A dark ground with one lit subject is the classic way to force attention onto that subject.

### A8. Colour grading - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_grading

- Grading began as continuity (shots must match) and is now used to build a stylised look and set mood through colour.
- Consistency across the whole piece depends on a fixed reference; a look is a system, not per-shot taste.

## B. The sequence

### B1. Continuity editing - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_editing

- Continuous sound laid across a change smooths it: an unbroken sound tells the viewer time and space did not jump.
- Match on action: the next view picks the action up exactly where the last one left it.
- Establishing views, the 180-degree rule and eyeline matches together keep space readable.

### B2. Cutting on action - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cutting_on_action

- Start an action in one view and finish it in the next; the movement bridges the change and hides small mismatches.
- A variant: the subject leaves the frame in one view and enters in the next.

### B3. 180-degree rule and screen direction - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/180-degree_rule

- Crossing the line flips left and right and disorients the viewer.
- A subject that exits left must enter the next view from the right to read as continuous travel.
- Exit and re-entry on the same side reads as time passing or as confusion.
- For us: travel direction is meaning. In the Arabic cut, time and progress read right to left, so the direction is mirrored on purpose and kept consistent.

### B4. Match cut - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_cut

- A graphic match joins two views by shape, colour or movement and makes the change visible on purpose.
- It is a comparison device: "this becomes that".

### B5. Jump cut - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jump_cut

- Removing a piece of one continuous view makes time jump abruptly; it breaks classical continuity and is a deliberate effect, not an accident.

### B6. Kuleshov effect - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuleshov_effect

- Viewers take meaning from two views in sequence more than from either alone; what follows a shot rewrites what it meant.

### B7. Walter Murch, editing - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Murch and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_the_Blink_of_an_Eye_(Murch_book)

- Murch judges every cut against six criteria ("the rule of six"), and the book argues emotion comes before technical neatness.
- Practical reading: a cut that is right for the feeling and the story beats one that is merely smooth.

### B8. Ad structure (AIDA) - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDA_(marketing)

- The viewer moves from awareness to interest to a favourable feeling to an action.
- Processing is both cognitive and emotional before anyone acts; a 40-60 s film needs a hook, a problem felt, a turn, a payoff and one call to action.

### B9. Film grammar - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_grammar

- Film has conventions viewers have learned (sizes, cuts, direction); breaking one should be a choice the viewer can feel.

## C. Voice, words on screen

### C1. Voice-over - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voice-over

- Synchronous narration, describing what is happening as it happens, is the most common form.
- Commercial and animation narration is an off-screen voice that carries the thread across images.

### C2. Subtitle timing and reading - https://www.bbc.co.uk/accessibility/forproducts/guides/subtitles/

- Reading rate guidance is 160-180 words per minute; leave a subtitle up at least about 0.3 s per word (a 4-word line needs about 1.2 s).
- At most two lines on a landscape frame.
- Break lines at natural phrase boundaries; consider the pace of speech and the picture when breaking.
- Avoid a subtitle straddling a shot change.

### C3. English timed text - https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-Timed-Text-Style-Guide

- When reading speed is a problem, cut words (reduce, delete, condense) rather than rush.
- Prefer a bottom-heavy two-line shape; never leave one or two words alone on the top line.

### C4. Arabic diacritics - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_diacritics

- Modern Arabic is written with consonant dots but normally without vowel marks; full marks are for religious texts, children's books and learners.
- Writers add a mark only where meaning would be ambiguous. This supports our rule: no visible tashkeel; marks live only in the voice text, and an ambiguous caption is rewritten rather than vowelled.

### C5. Bidirectional text - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bidirectional_text

- Mixed Arabic and Latin runs are ordered by character direction classes (strong, weak, neutral); neutral characters such as punctuation and brackets take direction from their neighbours, which is where mixed lines go wrong.
- Isolate every Latin run (product names, URLs, numbers with units) inside Arabic text.

### C6. Kinetic typography - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_typography

- Text presented over time with motion can carry an idea or an emotion, not only information.
- Early film type was static text switched by cuts; animated type lets the word itself act.

## D. Animation

### D1. The twelve principles - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve_basic_principles_of_animation

- Squash and stretch shows weight and impact, and volume must stay constant.
- Anticipation prepares the audience for an action (wind-up before the pitch) and makes the action read.
- Staging presents an idea so it is completely and unmistakably clear: placement, light and shadow, and camera all point at the one important thing.
- Slow in and slow out: things move less at the start and end of a move (easing).
- Arcs: natural motion follows curves; leaving the arc for no reason looks erratic.
- Follow-through and overlapping action: loose parts keep going after the body stops; parts move on different timings.
- Secondary action supports the main action and must never pull attention from it.
- Timing (frames per action) sets weight, physics, mood and reaction.
- Exaggeration is reality pushed further, still true to it; pose-to-pose suits staged, emotional scenes.

### D2. Storyboards and animatics - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storyboard

- An animatic is the storyboard's stills cut to a rough soundtrack, showing motion and timing before animation is made.
- It is where camera position, shot list and timing problems are caught; board and track are revised and the animatic re-cut until the board is final.
- Editing at the animatic stage saves animating scenes that would later be cut.

### D3. GSAP timeline - https://gsap.com/docs/v3/GSAP/Timeline/

- The position parameter places each tween absolutely, relative to the end (+=, -=) or at a label; overlap with "-=" is how beats breathe into each other.
- Labels name beats so a timeline can be retimed by moving a label, not every tween.

### D4. GSAP easing - https://gsap.com/docs/v3/Eases/

- Changing the ease changes the whole feel and personality of a move; power1 to power4 are increasing strengths of the same curve family.

## E. Explainer pedagogy (lighter)

### E1. Split attention - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_attention_effect

- When related information is split across a display, the viewer must integrate it in their head and learns less; integrated diagrams (label next to the thing) do better.

### E2. Modality effect - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modality_effect

- People learn better when pictures are paired with spoken narration than with on-screen text carrying the same words, because read text competes with the picture for the eyes.

### E3. Cognitive load - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load

- Working memory is limited; instructional design should cut extraneous load. Named effects include modality, split attention and worked example.

### E4. Tutorials - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explainer_video (redirects to Tutorial)

- A video tutorial is watched, not followed step by step; it should show one task and its result, not every control.

### E5. Multimedia learning - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multimedia_learning

- Balanced visual and verbal content, segmented into short parts, supports learning.
